## Responsible Governance Liberalism: The Liberal Path to Fiscal Discipline in the U.S. since the 1970s

*Dissertation Project (ASA Dissertation Development Research Improvement Grant)*

**Question**: Why did fiscal responsibility become a durable component of Democratic political identity precisely when Republicans were moving away from it?

Using large language models to analyze over 8 million congressional floor speeches alongside archival evidence, I trace how fiscal responsibility became a durable feature of Democratic political identity. I show that this endured not because Democrats became neoliberals, but because liberal Democrats articulated a version of fiscal responsibility — what I term "responsible governance liberalism" — that complemented rather than contradicted commitments to social protection and government activism. In addition, fiscal responsibility functioned as a "coalition magnet" in the Demcoratic Party, which draws together otherwise competing factions within the party around a shared governing vision and giving Democrats a language for criticizing Republican governing records. The dissertation traces the emergence of these conditions in the 1970s and 1980s, then examines their mobilization in two pivotal legislative episodes: the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 and the Statutory Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGO) Act of 2010.


## Tracing Politicization in U.S. Public Discourse, 1980-2024

*Conditionally Accepted at Sociological Science* [See pre-print here](https://osf.io/mwz2f/files/c863s)

**Question**: How has the boundary of the "political" in American public discourse changed over time?

While many argue that American society has become overly politicized, social scientists have yet to systematically measure “politicization”—the process by which issues are framed as political through their association with recognizable political divisions in
public discourse. Using word embedding models trained on 1.35 million newspaper articles, I develop a new measure of discursive politicization and trace its trajectory across 25 issue domains between 1980 and 2024. I find that politicization has increased substantially over the past four decades and expanded beyond traditional political issues into many areas of social life. The perceived center of political conflict has also shifted, with race and immigration, gender and family, and legal institutions becoming more politicized than economic issues. This paper challenges how we think about “politics” as a fundamental category in social science: rather than treating the meaning of politics as stable, I show how the boundary and content of politics are constantly redefined in different historical periods.


## Mapping the Relationship between Public and Personal Culture: A Computational Text Analysis of "Woke" in America

*Working Paper (with Jintae Bae, Sakeef Karim, and Milos Brocic)*

**Question**: How are public narratives around "wokeness" aligned or misaligned with personal understandings of it?

This project combines the American Culture War Survey with a computational analysis of five mainstream media outlets from 2015 to 2025 to examine the relationship between public narratives and personal culture around "woke." We find a significant mismatch between the "public" understanding of "woke," as reflected in elite newspaper coverage, and the "personal" understanding of the term, as reflected in our open-ended survey responses: many ordinary Americans today still interpret “woke” positively, as awareness of what is going on in society, while mainstream media overwhelmingly frames it negatively, through the lens of radical ideology or racial and gender identity conflict. But because most people encounter “woke” primarily through this negative media framing without an accurate understanding of their peers’ actual views, more than 70% of respondents believe others mostly use “woke” negatively, even though only 36% personally think it is a bad thing. We therefore conclude that “woke” is a clear demonstration of pluralistic ignorance, in which public discourse shapes the terrain of democratic debate in a way that misrepresents what citizens actually believe.
